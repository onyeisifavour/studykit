Question 1: Number Sets and Density Properties (10 marks)
(a) Proof that an irrational exists between any two distinct rationals (5 marks)

Proof:
Let p and q be distinct rational numbers with p < q.

Consider the interval (p, q) of length (q − p) > 0.

By the Density Theorem for Irrational Numbers, the set of irrational numbers is dense in ℝ. This means in any open interval, no matter how small, there exists at least one irrational number.

Since (p, q) is an open interval in ℝ, there must exist at least one irrational number r such that p < r < q.

[Alternative Constructive Proof]:
Let α = √2 / 100 (an irrational constant).
Consider r = p + α(q − p).
Since p and q are rational and α is irrational, r is irrational (sum of rational and irrational).
Since 0 < α(q − p) < (q − p), we have p < r < q. ∎

Marking: 1 mark for invoking density; 2 marks for logical structure; 2 marks for rigour/completeness.

(b) Construct an irrational between 0.3 and 0.4; verify irrationality (5 marks)

Construction:
Consider r = 0.3 + 0.05√2 = 0.3 + 0.05 × 1.41421... ≈ 0.3707...

Verification that r is irrational:
Assume r is rational. Then:
r = 0.3 + 0.05√2
r − 0.3 = 0.05√2

Therefore, r must be irrational. ∎

(c) Explain the place-value logic (3 marks)

Explanation:
In binary, each digit represents a power of 2: 2⁰, 2¹, 2², 2³, 2⁴, ...
In hexadecimal, each digit represents a power of 16.
Since 16 = 2⁴, one hexadecimal digit captures exactly 4 binary digits:

1 hex digit = 16¹ value
           = 2⁴ value
           = 4 binary digits combined
Grouping 4 consecutive binary digits (e.g., b₃b₂b₁b₀) gives:
b₃(2³) + b₂(2²) + b₁(2¹) + b₀(2⁰) = a value from 0 to 15, which is exactly one hexadecimal digit (0–F).

This one-to-one correspondence makes nibble grouping a valid positional shortcut.

Question 4: Constants, Redefinition, and Practical Implications (14 marks)
(a) Elementary charge, base unit dependency, and conceptual link (6 marks)

Which base unit depends on e?
The ampere (A) is defined via the elementary charge.

Conceptual link:
The ampere measures electric current, which is the flow of charge per unit time:
I = Q/t (current = charge/time)

In the 2019 SI redefinition:

The elementary charge e is fixed at exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs.

The coulomb (C) is then defined as the amount of charge transferred when 1 ampere flows for 1 second: 1 C = 1 A·s.

By fixing e, physicists can operationally define the ampere: 1 ampere is the current at which 1 elementary charge (1 e) is transferred per unit time interval (specifically, in a Josephson junction or similar quantum standard).

This breaks the historical circularity where ampere and coulomb were cross-defined. Now the ampere is anchored directly to a fundamental constant, ensuring reproducibility without relying on macroscopic standards.

(b) Speed of light, metre, second, and independence (5 marks)

How c affects definitions:
In the 2019 SI, the speed of light c is fixed at exactly 299,792,458 m/s.

Are metre and second now dependent or independent?

Second: fraction of a solar day, then related to caesium-133 hyperfine transition.

In modern SI: By fixing c, the relationship becomes:
1 metre = c × (1/299,792,458 second)

This means the metre and second are now operationally linked through c, though not mathematically redundant:

The second is defined independently via the caesium-133 frequency.

The metre is then derived from c and the second: 1 m = (299,792,458 s⁻¹) × 1 s.

Consequence: You cannot independently measure both c and the metre; fixing c constrains the metre's magnitude. However, both units remain essential because they measure fundamentally different phenomena (time vs. spatial extent), and fixing c ensures global consistency without requiring a physical standard for either.

(c) Advantage and disadvantage of fixing universal constants (3 marks)

Advantage:
Fundamental constants (e.g., h, c, e) are truly invariant across space, time, and conditions. They cannot degrade, be lost, or damaged (unlike the platinum-iridium kilogram). Basing units on these constants ensures that measurements made today, a century from now, or on another planet will remain consistent without recalibrating to a physical standard.

Disadvantage:
Defining units via fundamental constants requires advanced technology and quantum mechanics expertise (e.g., Josephson junctions for voltage standards, optical clocks for the second). Developing, maintaining, and distributing these standards is expensive and technically demanding. Historically, a physical artefact (however imperfect) was more accessible: any laboratory could hold a kilogram standard. Modern redefinition shifts burden to specialized national metrology institutes, raising barriers for developing nations or resource-limited institutions.
